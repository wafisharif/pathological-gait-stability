A Data-Driven Simulation Framework for Comparing Pathological Locomotion Stability
The goal of this project is to develop a simulation framework that uses public pathological gait datasets to develop locomotion controller variants for multiple clinical populations, and subsequently compare their stability through simulation. This project aims to investigate how characteristic movement patterns of different populations can influence stability when translated into a common environment.
The current populations of interest are healthy controls, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, ALS, and stroke. These populations were chosen in particular because they represent different mechanisms of impairment, and also because suitable datasets exist for each. For example, Parkinson's disease is often associated with altered gait rhythm/timing, Huntington's disease with irregular movement, ALS with weakness-related patterns, and stroke with asymmetry and unilateral impairment. These populations provide a broad range of abnormalities that can be studied within this framework.
One motivation for this project is that gait datasets and movement simulations are often used independently. Existing studies use gait datasets to describe pathological movement, while simulation studies frequently focus on locomotion control without grounding controller behavior in real gait data. As a result, we often know how different populations walk and how to simulate walking, but there is much less work connecting those areas to investigate how population-specific gait signatures influence simulated locomotor stability.
This gap is important because stability is not a single quantity. A controller that is stable under one metric may appear unstable under another. For instance, a controller may complete many strides without falling but still respond very poorly to perturbations. This project combines gait datasets, simulation, and multiple stability measures to help identify which aspects of pathological gait contribute most to instability and whether different stability metrics reveal different characteristics of movement.
The primary dataset for the populations will be the PhysioNet Gait Dynamics in Neurodegenerative Disease dataset, which includes healthy controls, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, and ALS. This dataset provides comparable variables, including: stride interval, swing interval, stance interval, and double-support interval, along with subject metadata including age, walking speed, and disease severity. For Parkinson's disease, the PhysioNet Gait in Parkinson's Disease dataset may be used as a supplementary source because it provides vertical ground reaction force measurements from sensors. For stroke, the main dataset will be a full-body motion capture dataset containing 138 able-bodied adults and 50 stroke survivors. A secondary PhysioNet stroke dataset may also be used if other pressure-based gait variables are needed.
The strongest common feature set across these datasets includes timing-based gait measures like stride time, stance time, swing time, double-support time, gait variability, walking speed, and left-right asymmetry. These variables will be the foundation of the analysis because they are available across multiple populations. More complex variables will be supplementary features and used only where they are available.
The first phase of the project will involve extracting population-specific gait signatures from the datasets. In this phase, we will identify the movement characteristics that differentiate each population from healthy controls, which is the baseline population. The second phase will involve turning these gait signatures into modifications of a baseline locomotion controller in MuJoCo. Training entirely new controllers from raw movement data proves to be very tedious and comes with its own nuances, so the project will begin with a baseline controller and create controller variants based on the observed characteristics. For example, the controller for Parkinson's disease will aim to show altered timing and increased variability, and so on.
An important consideration is that controller differences may not arise only from movement timing. Pathological gait may also involve different feedback mechanisms, delayed corrections, or higher levels of noise. So, the project will also explore whether controller variants should incorporate changes in feedback behavior in addition to kinematic modifications.
Once the controller variants have been developed, they will be evaluated within a common MuJoCo/MyoSuite simulation environment. Controllers will be tested under consistent conditions, including normal and perturbation scenarios, to evaluate how they respond to conditions.
A major focus of the project will be the use of multiple stability metrics. Traditional measures such as success rate, distance traveled, time before failure, and fall occurrence will be used alongside more established stability measures from literature. Some potential metrics include Lyapunov-based measures, Floquet-based measures, basin-of-support approaches, and total body angular momentum. If possible, more advanced approaches like uncontrolled manifold analysis will also be explored.
The expected outcome is that different population-specific controller variants will exhibit different stability profiles and different stability metrics will capture different aspects of those profiles. These findings would ideally suggest that stability is multidimensional and cannot be fully characterized by a single measure. More generally, the project will ideally provide a framework that connects gait datasets, locomotion simulation, and stability analysis in a way that can help researchers of the field better understand the relationship between pathological movement patterns and robustness in movement, additionally incorporating feedback and noise analyses.
